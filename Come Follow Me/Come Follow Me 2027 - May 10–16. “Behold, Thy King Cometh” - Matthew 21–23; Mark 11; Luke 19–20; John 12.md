# May 10–16. “Behold, Thy King Cometh”: Matthew 21–23; Mark 11; Luke 19–20; John 12

[Link](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-new-testament-2027/20?lang=eng)

It had been about three years since John the Baptist said, “I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Make straight the way of the Lord” ([[John 1.23|John 1:23]]). Now, as Jesus rode triumphantly into Jerusalem, that one voice had become many, crying, “Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord” ([[John 12.13|John 12:13]]). With branches from palm trees and even their own clothing, they lovingly and joyfully paved His way into their beloved holy city.

But John wasn’t talking just about giving the King a smooth path to tread into Jerusalem. He was talking about opening the way for Him to enter our lives—so He can drive out corruption and heal us (see [[Matthew 21.12|Matthew 21:12–14]][[Matthew 21.13|]][[Matthew 21.14|]]). In that sense, Jesus’s triumphal entry is ongoing. The voices crying “Hosanna” will continue to multiply until that great future day when “a great multitude” will gather “of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, … clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands” ([[Revelation 7.9|Revelation 7:9]]).

### The Lord judges not by the outward appearance but by the heart.

In Jesus’s time, many people hated tax collectors, or publicans, assuming they were dishonest and stole from the people. Because Zacchaeus, the chief tax collector, was wealthy, he probably faced even more suspicion. But Jesus saw beyond that and looked at Zacchaeus’s heart.

- What do you notice about Zacchaeus’s heart in [[Luke 19.1|Luke 19:1–10]][[Luke 19.2|]][[Luke 19.3|]][[Luke 19.4|]][[Luke 19.5|]][[Luke 19.6|]][[Luke 19.7|]][[Luke 19.8|]][[Luke 19.9|]][[Luke 19.10|]]? What evidence do you see of his commitment to the Savior?

- What do you think Zacchaeus learned about the Savior from his experience?

- What does the Savior understand about you that might not be obvious to others?

### Jesus Christ is my Savior and my King.

When Jesus entered Jerusalem shortly before completing His Atonement, faithful disciples honored Him as their King. They anointed Him, spread clothes and palm branches on His path, and praised Him. But Jesus, of course, was different from most kings. He had come to conquer not with swords and armies but with humble sacrifice.

These resources can deepen your understanding of this event that began the last week of the Savior’s life:

- An ancient prophecy of the triumphal entry: [[Zechariah 9.9|Zechariah 9:9]]

### I should give “God the things that are God’s.”

### The two great commandments are to love God and love my neighbor.

The Savior answered difficult questions with simplicity and power, even when facing opposition. An example can be found in His response to the question “Which is the great commandment in the law?” ([[Matthew 22.36|Matthew 22:36]]).

Have you ever faced a situation where it felt difficult to keep both of the two great commandments? What do you do in those situations? As you ponder this, look at what Elder Gary E. Stevenson said in “An Interdependency” in his message “[[2024 April - Bridging the Two Great Commandments.md|Bridging the Two Great Commandments]]” (*Liahona*, May 2024, 107; see also the video “The Heart of the Gospel: Love God and Neighbor,” Gospel Library).

You might also sketch a bridge, as Elder Stevenson suggests, labeling the towers with things you can do “to live the first and second commandments with balance and equal devotion to both.” You can find ideas in “Love God, Love Your Neighbor,” in *For the Strength of Youth: A Guide for Making Choices*, 28–32.

See also “The Greatest Commandment” (video), Gospel Library.

### Jesus condemns hypocrisy.

In a sense, the scribes and Pharisees were the opposite of Zacchaeus—they appeared righteous on the outside, but their hearts weren’t right. Jesus called them “hypocrites”—a word of Greek origin meaning “actor” or “pretender.”

In [[Matthew 23.1|Matthew 23]][[Matthew 23.2|]][[Matthew 23.3|]][[Matthew 23.4|]][[Matthew 23.5|]][[Matthew 23.6|]][[Matthew 23.7|]][[Matthew 23.8|]][[Matthew 23.9|]][[Matthew 23.10|]][[Matthew 23.11|]][[Matthew 23.12|]][[Matthew 23.13|]][[Matthew 23.14|]][[Matthew 23.15|]][[Matthew 23.16|]][[Matthew 23.17|]][[Matthew 23.18|]][[Matthew 23.19|]][[Matthew 23.20|]][[Matthew 23.21|]][[Matthew 23.22|]][[Matthew 23.23|]][[Matthew 23.24|]][[Matthew 23.25|]][[Matthew 23.26|]][[Matthew 23.27|]][[Matthew 23.28|]][[Matthew 23.29|]][[Matthew 23.30|]][[Matthew 23.31|]][[Matthew 23.32|]][[Matthew 23.33|]][[Matthew 23.34|]][[Matthew 23.35|]][[Matthew 23.36|]][[Matthew 23.37|]][[Matthew 23.38|]][[Matthew 23.39|]], the Savior used several metaphors to describe hypocrisy. Consider marking or listing these metaphors and noting what they teach about hypocrisy. You might also ponder your reasons for doing spiritual things.

- What is the difference between hypocrisy and the human weaknesses we all deal with as we try to live the gospel?

- What are you inspired to do differently because of the Savior’s teachings in [[Matthew 23.1|Matthew 23]][[Matthew 23.2|]][[Matthew 23.3|]][[Matthew 23.4|]][[Matthew 23.5|]][[Matthew 23.6|]][[Matthew 23.7|]][[Matthew 23.8|]][[Matthew 23.9|]][[Matthew 23.10|]][[Matthew 23.11|]][[Matthew 23.12|]][[Matthew 23.13|]][[Matthew 23.14|]][[Matthew 23.15|]][[Matthew 23.16|]][[Matthew 23.17|]][[Matthew 23.18|]][[Matthew 23.19|]][[Matthew 23.20|]][[Matthew 23.21|]][[Matthew 23.22|]][[Matthew 23.23|]][[Matthew 23.24|]][[Matthew 23.25|]][[Matthew 23.26|]][[Matthew 23.27|]][[Matthew 23.28|]][[Matthew 23.29|]][[Matthew 23.30|]][[Matthew 23.31|]][[Matthew 23.32|]][[Matthew 23.33|]][[Matthew 23.34|]][[Matthew 23.35|]][[Matthew 23.36|]][[Matthew 23.37|]][[Matthew 23.38|]][[Matthew 23.39|]] and [[Luke 20.45|Luke 20:45–47]][[Luke 20.46|]][[Luke 20.47|]]?

> **You don’t need to cover everything.** If you are a teacher, you can look for activity ideas in both “Ideas for Learning at Home and at Church” and “Ideas for Teaching Children,” based on the needs of the people you teach. But don’t try to fit too much into one class. You don’t have to cover everything in order to touch someone’s heart—often one or two key points are enough. The Spirit will help you identify which principles, stories, or scriptures will be most meaningful.

### The temple is a sacred place.

- To introduce [[Matthew 21.12|Matthew 21:12–14]][[Matthew 21.13|]][[Matthew 21.14|]], you and your children could look at a picture of a temple and share with each other some words that describe the house of the Lord. Or you could sing together “[I Love to See the Temple](https://churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/childrens-songbook/i-love-to-see-the-temple?lang=eng)” (*Children’s Songbook*, 95) and talk about why you love the temple. Then you could look at things that would stain or damage the temple and talk about why we should keep these things away from the Lord’s house. This could help your children understand what happened in [[Matthew 21.12|Matthew 21:12–14]][[Matthew 21.13|]][[Matthew 21.14|]]. How can we help Jesus keep the temple holy or sacred?

[[Matthew 23.25|Matthew 23:25–28]][[Matthew 23.26|]][[Matthew 23.27|]][[Matthew 23.28|]]
### Jesus wants me to be righteous for the right reasons, not just to impress others.

- It might be fun to illustrate [[Matthew 23.25|Matthew 23:25–28]][[Matthew 23.26|]][[Matthew 23.27|]][[Matthew 23.28|]] by showing your children a cup that is clean on the outside but dirty on the inside. You could also talk about the meaning of the word *hypocrite* from the [[BD Hypocrite|Bible Dictionary]]: “One who pretends to be religious when he is not.” How is the dirty cup like a hypocrite? How can we ensure we are clean and pure on the inside?
